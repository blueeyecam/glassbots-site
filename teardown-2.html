Your AI is shouting at you.
That's why you stopped listening.

GlassBots · August 2026 · 5 minute read

A monitoring system on a production AI platform had a bug. A price-tracking alert, when a stock was near a trigger, would re-send the same "price is approaching trigger" message on every scheduled run — for as long as the price stayed near it. The function had no state, no memory, no cooldown. It didn't know it had already told you.

Over fourteen days, it sent 786 messages. That was 81% of all alert traffic on the platform. 96% of them came in unbroken runs of three or more — the same line, repeated, over and over, until the recipient's brain filed the entire channel as noise.

The fix was easy: a cooldown, a state flag, a check that says "I already sent this." Five lines of code. But the fix isn't the story. The story is that nobody noticed for two weeks.

Noisy alerts are worse than no alerts

Here's the mechanism that makes this worse than silence: when an alert system is noisy, the human learns to ignore the channel. Not the specific alert — the channel. Telegram, email, the dashboard, the Slack bot. Once your brain has classified a channel as "mostly noise," you stop checking it. Then the real alert — the one that matters — arrives on the same channel. And you miss it.

A monitoring system that sends 786 duplicate alerts is not over-reporting. It is training its operator to ignore it.

This is a security principle dressed as an operations lesson: an alarm that fires constantly is not an alarm. It's wallpaper. The most dangerous monitoring system is not the one that fails silently — it's the one that succeeds so loudly that real signal drowns.

Why the AI did this to itself

The monitoring function was stateless. It had no memory of what it had already said. Every scheduled run was a fresh start: "is the price near the trigger? Yes. Alert." It didn't know it had alerted 47 times yesterday. It didn't know the recipient had stopped reading.

This is the same failure that makes AI agents stall silently — no definition of "already done." The monitoring version is louder but equally blind: no definition of "already said."

The three rules that fix it

  • Cooldown. The same alert cannot fire again within a time window. If the price is still near the trigger in 30 minutes, that's the same alert. Suppress it.
  • State change. An alert fires only when something changes — "price crossed the trigger" not "price is near the trigger." Proximity is continuous; crossings are events.
  • Repetition is the alarm. The monitoring system itself needs a watchdog: if any alert fires 3+ times in an unbroken run, that's a finding. Not "the system is working" — "the system is stuck."

The third rule is the one almost nobody implements. It requires the system to monitor its own monitoring — and to treat its own repetition as a fault, not as evidence of vigilance.

Ask your own system, right now

  1. How many alerts did you receive from your AI system last week?
  2. How many of those did you actually read past the subject line?
  3. Is there a channel you've muted or filter to a folder you never open?
